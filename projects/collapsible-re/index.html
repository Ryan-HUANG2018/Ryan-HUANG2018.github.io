Transition to chaos in a collapsible channel

Flow through a two-dimensional channel in which a segment of both walls is elastic, a model of the Starling resistor and of collapsible vessels such as compressed coronary arteries. Over 100 ≤ Re ≤ 3000 and wall-to-fluid mass ratios 0.3 ≤ M ≤ 100, the walls can be steady or oscillate periodically, through period-doubling, quasi-periodically or chaotically. Switch between four cases at M = 1 and a heavy-wall case (Re = 500, M = 20) with large-amplitude oscillation to compare the jet through the throat and the vortices downstream. From our 2021 Journal of Fluid Mechanics paper.

100–3000

Setup

A two-dimensional channel of width D has rigid walls upstream (Lu = 5D) and downstream (Ld = 30D). Between them, a segment of length L = 5D on both walls is replaced by an elastic beam, loaded by an external pressure pe. A Poiseuille flow with mean velocity U0 enters on the left, and the outlet pressure is pd. The elastic walls are initially flat and clamped to the rigid walls at both ends. Extending the classical one-sided collapsible channel to two sides makes the model closer to real vessels and airways, and to the Starling resistor used in experiments.

Scaled by U0, D and the fluid density ρ0, the system has five governing parameters:

Parameter Definition Values studied
Reynolds number Re = U0D / ν 100–3000
Mass ratio M = ρs / (ρ0D) 0.3–100
Stretching stiffness Ks = Eh / (ρ0U02D) 2400
Bending stiffness Kb = EI / (ρ0U02D3) Kb / Ks ≈ 10−5 (h = 1 % of D)
External pressure Pe = (pe − pd) / (ρ0U02) 1–10 (1.95 for the regime map)

The fluid is solved with a D2Q9 multi-relaxation-time lattice Boltzmann method, and the walls with a geometrically nonlinear beam equation discretised by finite differences. An iterative feedback immersed boundary method couples the two: it reduces velocity slip on the walls and improves mass conservation in confined flows. The solver was validated against the one-sided collapsible channel of Luo et al. (2008), and a grid of 4000 × 140 lattice nodes (Δx = 0.01D) was chosen after a mesh-independence study.

Results

Regime map. Each case is classified from the wall-motion time history, its power spectrum, the phase portrait of the wall mid-point and the dominant Lyapunov exponent. Chaos appears mainly at high Re with light walls, or at low Re with heavy walls. At M = 50 and 100 only chaotic states are found.

Routes to chaos at M = 1. The channel is steady at Re = 100. A supercritical Hopf bifurcation then gives a period-1 limit-cycle oscillation at Re = 150–200. The walls are steady again for 250 ≤ Re ≤ 500, become chaotic at Re = 550, show period-doubling at Re = 600 and are quasi-periodic at Re = 650, before returning to chaos at Re = 800. This second path follows the quasi-periodic (Ruelle–Takens–Newhouse) route. At M = 3 the system instead reaches chaos at Re = 200 by period-doubling, the classical Feigenbaum route.

The dominant Lyapunov exponents confirm the classification (M = 1, Ks = 2400, Pe = 1.95):

Re Motion state Lyapunov exponent λ
100 steady —
200 periodic 0.0021
500 steady —
550 chaotic 0.0385
600 period-doubling 0.0076
650 quasi-periodic 0.0124
800 chaotic 0.0417

Heavier walls at Re = 500. With Ks = 2400 and Pe = 1.95, the oscillation amplitude grows with the mass ratio. The walls end up steady for M ≤ 1, oscillate with an amplitude below 0.01 at M = 2, and switch from a small-amplitude mode at M = 5 to a large-amplitude mode at M = 10. At M = 20 (the last case in the player) the walls bulge out strongly upstream, then bounce back to a strongly collapsed state, yet the two walls and the shed vortex pairs stay symmetric. At the most collapsed instant a strong central jet entrains the earlier vortex pairs. This resembles the jet propulsion of a cephalopod more than the jet from a nozzle.

Mechanisms.

  • Increasing Re breaks the symmetry of the jet through a supercritical pitchfork bifurcation, but this alone does not make the walls oscillate: symmetry breaking is not a prerequisite for self-excited oscillations.
  • The oscillations are driven by the unbalanced transmural pressure, the wall inertia and shear-layer instabilities in the vorticity waves downstream of the throat.
  • Period-doubling, quasi-periodic and chaotic states come with vortex pairing and merging, and with interactions between the vortex rows on the upper and lower walls.
  • For a heavy wall, travelling-wave flutter along the elastic walls breaks their symmetry and triggers chaos. High mass ratio and external pressure produce large-amplitude oscillations.
