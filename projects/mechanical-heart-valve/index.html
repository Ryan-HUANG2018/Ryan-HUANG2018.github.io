Mechanical heart valve leaflet in pulsatile flow

A rigid mechanical heart-valve leaflet, hinged at the entrance of an aortic sinus, is driven open and shut by a pulsatile inflow. Its motion is not prescribed: the leaflet rotates under the torque of the blood flow. This is the benchmark experiment of Stijnen et al. (2004), which I used to validate my immersed boundary–lattice Boltzmann solver for heart valves before applying it to an idealised model of supravalvular aortic stenosis. From our 2024 Australasian Fluid Mechanics Conference paper.

REYNOLDS NUMBER
750
STROUHAL NUMBER
0.055
LEAFLET
Rigid, l = 1.07D
METHOD
IB–LBM (D2Q9 MRT)

Setup

Fluid flows through a two-dimensional channel of height D = 2 cm with a semicircular sinus on one wall, the shape of the aortic root behind a valve. A rigid leaflet of length l = 1.07D, thickness h = 0.05D and density 1100 kg/m³ is hinged at the start of the sinus (point O), with an initial opening angle of 22°. A pulsatile, spatially uniform velocity enters on the left, with a peak of Umax = 0.15 m/s. This gives Re = Ul/ν = 750 and a Strouhal number St = D/(UmaxTp) = 0.055.

The leaflet turns only under the fluid torque To about its hinge:

\[J_o\,\ddot{\theta}(t) = T_o(t), \qquad J_o = \tfrac{1}{3}(\rho_s l h)\,l^{2}\]

The blood (density 1090 kg/m³, kinematic viscosity 4 × 10⁻⁶ m²/s) is solved with a D2Q9 multi-relaxation-time lattice Boltzmann method, and the leaflet equation with finite differences. A diffused-interface immersed boundary method couples the two in both directions.

Results

Validation. Over one cardiac cycle, the opening angle agrees closely with the simulations of Stijnen et al. (2004) and Li et al. (2022). All simulations open slightly wider than the experiment, which Stijnen et al. attribute to fluid leaking over the top of the valve between the strips of adhesive tape that held the leaflet. At t/T = 0.37, with the leaflet pushed wide open, a large vortex rolls up at its trailing edge, as observed in the experiment.

Application: supravalvular aortic stenosis. Supravalvular aortic stenosis is a rare congenital narrowing of the ascending aorta just above the aortic valve. It alters how the valve moves and creates a large pressure drop across the narrowing. I built an idealised, axisymmetric model from the aortic root to the ascending aorta:

  • aortic diameter D = 2 cm, with a sinus cavity of radius R = D;
  • two rigid leaflets of length 0.57D, initially open at 40°;
  • a cosine-shaped stenosis of length 2D that reduces the diameter by 70%; and
  • Re = UD/ν = 200, St = 0.055, and a grid of 0.01D in a 35D × 3.4D domain.

After about four cycles, the leaflet motion repeats from cycle to cycle. The valve stays open for more than half of each cycle. While it is open, the pressure in the sinus is nearly uniform, but a strong negative pressure develops downstream of the stenosis, where a high-speed jet forms. A vortex shed from the leaflets hits the junction between the sinus and the downstream aortic wall.
